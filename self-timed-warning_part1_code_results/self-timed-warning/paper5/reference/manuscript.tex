\title [mode = title]{Evaluating end-of-life alarms: observation time, alarm timing, maintenance cost and transfer}
\begin{abstract}
Remaining-useful-life models are mostly scored at evaluator-chosen times, whereas replacement is triggered once, by an alarm. On eight run-to-failure datasets we separate long-range prediction, near-failure estimation, alarm timing and maintenance cost for end-of-life alarms scored by an asymmetric window. With matched training and cross-fitted calibration, a fixed-time model placed 24.8\% of estimates in the window at half of life and 85.3\% at 90\% of life on PHM08; a self-timed recipe reached 91.7\%, no more than its regressor at 90\% of life, and FD004 showed the same pattern. A constant estimate at the recipe's alarm times scored within 3.6 points of the model's estimate, so the window mainly rewards timing. Under the decision-oriented metric of Kamariotis et al., re-implemented with their 10-cycle grid, their probability-threshold policy on our regressor beat our window-tuned alarm on FD001 (\textit{M} = 5.69\% vs 8.08\%) but lost on FD002 and FD003; none won everywhere. With fully nested preprocessing, the recipe was correct for 95.1\% of battery cells within batches and 79.7\% with a batch held out (63.4--84.6\% across time scales), while a capacity-trend baseline reached 98.4\% and cost less in most settings. The recipe reached 68.7\% on N-CMAPSS and failed on bearings (0 of 17).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

Data-driven prognostics estimates the remaining useful life (RUL) of a component from its condition-monitoring history \cite{lei2018,si2011}. On the standard turbofan benchmarks, estimates are usually scored at evaluator-chosen times: the last cycle of truncated test trajectories, every cycle, or fixed fractions of life \cite{ramasso2014}. A replacement decision, however, is taken once, at a time the decision rule itself chooses. Four quantities that are easily conflated then need to be kept apart. \textbf{Long-range prediction} is the accuracy of an estimate made far from failure. \textbf{Near-failure estimation} is its accuracy when little life is left. \textbf{Alarm timing} is the distribution of the remaining life at the moment the policy acts. \textbf{Maintenance-policy performance} is the long-run cost of the replacements and failures that the alarms produce. A good result on one does not imply a good result on another, and a single score can mix several of them.

This paper studies one operational score that mixes them: an asymmetric acceptance window for a single end-of-life alarm. An alarm is accepted if the true remaining life at the alarm lies between 2 cycles below and 13 cycles above the reported estimate, so that late alarms are tolerated far less than early ones. A policy observes each unit cycle by cycle, stays silent while the unit looks healthy, raises one alarm with a remaining-life estimate, and is scored at that alarm; a unit that fails without an alarm counts as late. Alarm-based maintenance is established. de Pater et al. \cite{depater2022} trigger maintenance scheduling from alarms on probabilistic RUL prognostics, and Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024} evaluate prognostic algorithms, and tune the policies built on them, by the increase in long-run maintenance cost that they induce relative to perfect prognostics. Their policies act on repeated probabilistic predictions at fixed decision times. What is less understood is what a window score of this kind actually rewards, whether optimising it yields good maintenance decisions, and how window-tuned alarms fare when they are judged by such a decision-oriented metric.

We use a deliberately simple recipe built from standard parts: a gradient-boosted regressor with a capped target and a smoothed, shifted and debounced threshold trigger, all selected by nested cross-validation. The recipe is a vehicle for the evaluation, not its contribution. The experiments answer four questions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item[(Q1)] How do long-range prediction, near-failure estimation, alarm timing and maintenance-policy performance differ, and how much of an apparent advantage of a self-timed alarm over fixed-time prediction is due to each, when training data and calibration information are matched?
\item[(Q2)] What does the asymmetric alarm window reward, and what can its score conceal?
\item[(Q3)] When does optimising window accuracy produce useful maintenance policies, and how do window-tuned alarms compare with alarms tuned directly to cost and, under the metric \textit{M} of Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024}, with their probability-threshold policies?
\item[(Q4)] What happens when evaluation moves from validation within familiar conditions to a held-out test batch or to a different asset type?
\end{itemize}

The contributions are the answers to these questions on eight run-to-failure datasets, together with the evaluation protocol that produces them. Four of them are new:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item[(i)] An observation-time decomposition of the contrast between fixed-time and self-timed evaluation under matched training data and cross-fitted calibration. It separates the observation time, the calibration, the target and feature design and the trigger; on PHM08, and again on FD004, most of the contrast is due to the observation time, and self-timing adds no measurable accuracy.
\item[(ii)] Evidence that an asymmetric acceptance window mostly measures alarm timing. A constant estimate at the recipe's alarm times scored within a few percentage points of the model's own estimate on each of the seven datasets on which it was run; a health-indicator alarm scored far lower, even when its indicator was calibrated to cycles and given the recipe's trigger (except on N-CMAPSS); and the score conceals the notice actually given.
\item[(iii)] An analysis of transfer to a held-out test batch and to other asset types, with a common physical task and fully nested preprocessing. On lithium-ion cells the recipe lost accuracy on a held-out batch under every time-scale convention examined, while a capacity-trend baseline was more accurate and cheaper; the recipe was weak on N-CMAPSS and failed on rolling bearings.
\item[(iv)] A conversion to, and comparison with, the decision-oriented metric \textit{M} of Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024}: the exact relation between our cost ratio and \textit{M}, the effect of their decision grid, and their policies 1 and 3 re-implemented on our regressor. Under their conventions the ranking depended on the dataset and on how the failure probability was built: at the primary cost ratio the lowest \textit{M} was reached by their policy 1 (P-res) on FD001 (5.69\%), our window-tuned alarm on FD002 (4.85\%), their policy 1 (P-cls) on FD003 (3.46\%) and our window-tuned alarm on FD004 (8.44\%), and our alarms beat their policy 3 on all four datasets. On FD001, the dataset of their study, our alarms did not match their policy 1.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:2}

\subsection{Remaining-life prediction and its evaluation}

The C-MAPSS simulations and the PHM08 challenge data \cite{saxena2008} are the standard engine benchmarks. The three winning PHM08 approaches were a similarity-based method \cite{wang2008}, a recurrent network \cite{heimes2008} and an ensemble of neural networks filtered by a Kalman filter \cite{peel2008}. Later work moved to long short-term memory \cite{zheng2017} and convolutional networks \cite{li2018}, usually with a remaining-life target capped near 125 cycles. N-CMAPSS adds realistic flight profiles \cite{ariaschao2021}. On tabular inputs, tree ensembles remain strong \cite{grinsztajn2022,friedman2001}. Severson et al. \cite{severson2019} cycled commercial lithium-ion cells to failure, and the PRONOSTIA platform produced run-to-failure vibration records of rolling bearings \cite{nectoux2012}. Ramasso and Saxena \cite{ramasso2014} showed how strongly reported scores depend on the choice of cut-off points and metrics. Saxena et al. \cite{saxena2010} introduced metrics that follow a prediction over time, such as the prognostic horizon and $\alpha$--$\lambda$ accuracy, and asymmetric losses that penalise late predictions are common \cite{rengasamy2020}.

\subsection{From prognostics to maintenance decisions}

Age replacement, which uses no condition information, is the classical benchmark for any condition-based policy \cite{barlow1960,dejonge2020}. Degradation models with Bayesian updating yield residual-life distributions \cite{gebraeel2005}, and dynamic predictive-maintenance frameworks act on predicted failure probabilities \cite{nguyen2019,mitici2023} or learn policies from probabilistic prognostics by reinforcement learning \cite{lee2023}. de Pater et al. \cite{depater2022} propose a fleet-level scheduling framework for aircraft in which periodically updated RUL prognostics of a convolutional network trigger alarms, maintenance is scheduled only after an alarm, a safety factor guards against prognostic error, and an integer linear program assigns aircraft to maintenance slots. Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024} propose a metric that assesses data-driven prognostic algorithms by their effect on downstream predictive-maintenance decisions: the relative increase in long-run expected maintenance cost per unit time, under a given decision setting and policy, compared with perfect prognostics, estimated from run-to-failure data. Decisions are taken at discrete times \textit{t}\textsubscript{k} = \textit{k}$\Delta$\textit{T}, and the perfect reference replaces each unit at the last decision time before its failure. A heuristic policy replaces when the predicted probability of failure within the next interval exceeds a threshold, and two further policies use the whole predictive distribution of the remaining life. They use the metric to compare algorithms, to optimise the parameters of these policies and to tune algorithm hyperparameters, on C-MAPSS FD001 split into 80 training and 20 test engines, and they note that an evaluation on 20 units is subject to significant statistical uncertainty. Xie et al. \cite{xie2025}, in a preprint, train the prognostic model itself against downstream maintenance cost on simulated turbofan (C-MAPSS) data.

\subsection{Position of this study}

The present study differs from these in what is evaluated. de Pater et al. \cite{depater2022} design an alarm-triggered scheduling system and evaluate its fleet cost; we take the alarm rule as given and ask what a common acceptance criterion for single alarms measures. Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024} recommend scoring prognostics by decision cost; we re-implement their metric and test our alarms under their conventions against their policies built on the same regressor (Section 7), and we use the same kind of cost evaluation to test whether tuning to an operational window is an adequate substitute for tuning to cost. Neither of those studies is designed to separate observation time, calibration, target design and alarm timing under matched training data, or to examine what happens to window accuracy and cost when a whole test batch is held out. Because such comparisons are easily biased by model selection on evaluation data \cite{varma2006} and by leakage \cite{kapoor2023}, every setting here is selected on training units only. Three datasets were analysed under protocols fixed in advance \cite{nosek2018}, with the limitations stated in Section 5.3.

\section{Problem formulation}\label{sec:3}

\subsection{First-warning task}

A unit \textit{i} is observed at cycles \textit{t} = 1, \ldots{}, \textit{L}\textsubscript{i}, where \textit{L}\textsubscript{i} is its life, unknown in service. The true remaining life at cycle \textit{t} is \textit{r}\textsubscript{t} = \textit{L}\textsubscript{i} \textminus{} \textit{t}. A warning policy maps the history up to \textit{t} to either \textit{silent} or \textit{warn}, together with an estimate $\hat{y}$\textsubscript{t}. The first-warning time is

\begin{equation}
t^{*} = \min\{\, t < L_i : \text{the policy warns at } t \,\},
\end{equation}

and the error of the warning is \textit{e} = $\hat{y}$\textsubscript{t*} \textminus{} \textit{r}\textsubscript{t*}. A warning is \textbf{correct} if \textminus{}13 $\le$ \textit{e} $\le$ +2, that is, exactly when

\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{t^*} - 2 \le r_{t^*} \le \hat{y}_{t^*} + 13.
\end{equation}

It is \textbf{too early} if \textit{e} < \textminus{}13 and \textbf{too late} if \textit{e} > +2; a unit that fails without a warning is \textbf{missed} and counted with the late outcomes. The primary score is the share of units whose first warning is correct (the in-window rate). We also report the late-or-missed and too-early rates, the true remaining life at the alarm (the notice actually given), and the share of alarms raised with \textit{r}\textsubscript{t*} $\ge$ 13. Only those alarms can be too early, unless the estimate is negative, so a policy that warned only in the last few cycles would meet the window trivially. The window was fixed by an operator's requirement before this study; its early limit coincides with the early constant of the PHM08 score \cite{saxena2008}, and its late limit is much stricter than that score's late constant of 10.

The warning level \textit{H} expresses how much notice is wanted: the policy warns when it believes that about \textit{H} cycles are left. \textit{H} = 20 is the primary level, designated on PHM08 as the smallest of \{10, 15, 20, 30, 40\} at which more than 90\% of alarms were raised with at least 13 cycles left (Section 5.3).

\subsection{Four quantities, and when evaluation leaks}

We separate (1) \textbf{long-range prediction}, the accuracy of $\hat{y}$\textsubscript{t} at times chosen independently of the policy, for example a fixed fraction of life; (2) \textbf{near-failure estimation}, the accuracy of $\hat{y}$\textsubscript{t} when \textit{r}\textsubscript{t} is small, for example at the cycle where \textit{r}\textsubscript{t} = \textit{H}; (3) \textbf{alarm timing}, the distribution of \textit{r}\textsubscript{t*} produced by the policy; and (4) \textbf{maintenance-policy performance}, the cost rate of Section 3.3. A fixed fraction of life is not known in service, but evaluating a model retrospectively at such a time is not leakage. It would be leakage if the fraction, the life or a rule derived from them were given to the model as an input or used to train or select it; the fixed-time models of Section 6.1 received none of these. Conversely, the first-warning task is not easier merely because the policy chooses its time: the choice must be made from the history alone, and a late or missed alarm is a failure.

\subsection{Maintenance cost}

Alarms are priced in a renewal--reward model \cite{barlow1960}. An alarm at cycle \textit{t}\textsuperscript{*} schedules a planned replacement $\ell$ cycles later. If \textit{r}\textsubscript{t*} > $\ell$ the replacement happens at \textit{t}\textsuperscript{*} + $\ell$ and costs 1; otherwise, or if no alarm is raised, the unit fails at \textit{L} and the failure costs $\rho$. An alarm with exactly $\ell$ cycles left counts as a failure. Units are replaced by new ones, so the long-run cost per cycle is estimated by the total cost divided by the total cycles of use over the test units. We use lead times $\ell$ $\in$ \{0, 5, 10, 15\} cycles for the engines and \{0, 15, 30, 45\} charge--discharge cycles for the batteries, and cost ratios $\rho$ $\in$ \{5, 10, 20, 50\}. The ratios are illustrative, not measured. Optimal age replacement replaces every unit at the age \textit{T} that minimises the cost rate on the training lives; it needs no lead time because the replacement age is known in advance. Perfect foresight (replacement at \textit{L} \textminus{} 1, cost 1) and run-to-failure bound the range, and every cost rate is reported relative to perfect foresight. With $\ell$ = 0 this ratio is closely related to the metric \textit{M} of Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024}, but it equals 1 + \textit{M} only when decisions can be taken at every cycle; Section 7 gives the exact relation for a coarser decision grid.

\subsection{Physical units}

For the battery and bearing data, whose lives are several times longer in their native units than engine lives, the models work in blocks of $\kappa$ consecutive charge--discharge cycles or vibration snapshots (Section 5.2). The task itself is defined in physical units: a warning level, an acceptance window and lead times in cycles (or snapshots). For the batteries the task is \textit{H} = 60 cycles and the window [\textminus{}39, +6] cycles, the engine task scaled by three; for the bearings it is \textit{H} = 140 snapshots and [\textminus{}91, +14] snapshots. A model with block length $\kappa$ uses the warning level, window, lead times, caps and shifts divided by $\kappa$, so that every acceptance decision, alarm cost and setting has the same meaning in physical units whatever $\kappa$ is; only the smoothing span and the debounce are counted in blocks. Alarms can only be raised at block ends, and the remaining life of a block is that at its last cycle; the remaining life at the alarm and the cycles of use are reported in cycles. Two battery analyses keep the task in blocks of $\kappa$ cycles, with $\kappa$ from the outer training cells (window [\textminus{}13, +2] blocks, warning levels in blocks): the timing controls and the shift study (Section 6.2). We label them convention B. Every other battery result, including the cost analysis, uses the fully nested preprocessing of Section 5.2 with the task in cycles (convention C, the primary convention). We write ``unit'' for an engine, cell or bearing.

\section{The recipe and the policies compared}\label{sec:4}

Fig. 1 shows the self-timed warning (STW) recipe. Its online part runs at every cycle of a unit in service; its offline part fixes four settings $\theta$ = (\textit{c}, \textit{m}, \textit{k}, \textit{s}) on training units only.

\begin{figure*}[!tbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{img/Fig1_STW_architecture.png}
\caption{The self-timed warning (STW) recipe. Online (top): regime-normalised sensors are encoded causally and mapped to an estimate $\hat{y}$\textsubscript{t}, which is smoothed and shifted to $\tilde{y}$\textsubscript{t}; an alarm is raised the first time $\tilde{y}$ stays at or below \textit{H} for \textit{k} cycles. Offline (bottom): $\theta$ = (\textit{c}, \textit{m}, \textit{k}, \textit{s}) is chosen on inner out-of-fold predictions of the training units and frozen; test units are scored once. Inset: the scoring window for \textit{e} = $\tilde{y}$\textsubscript{t*} \textminus{} \textit{r}\textsubscript{t*}.}

\textbf{Normalisation and features.} Each sensor is z-scored within its operating regime with statistics of the training units, and sensors that are constant within any regime are dropped (15 sensors remain on PHM08, FD001 and FD002, 16 on FD003 and FD004; N-CMAPSS uses the six temperature and shaft-speed channels fixed in its protocol). At cycle \textit{t} the encoder uses cycles 1, \ldots{}, \textit{t} only: an early-life baseline (the mean of the first min(20, \textit{t}) cycles), exponentially weighted means with spans 10 and 30 of the baseline-removed signals, least-squares slopes over the last 15 and 40 cycles, and the age \textit{t} (Supplementary Section S4 checks causality).

\textbf{Regressor.} A histogram gradient-boosted regressor \cite{pedregosa2011} with absolute-error loss (500 iterations, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, at least 40 samples per leaf, seed 0) is trained on all cycles of the training units with the target

\begin{equation}
y_t = \min( r_t, c ), \qquad c \in \{40, 60, 90\}.
\end{equation}

Capping the target is standard \cite{heimes2008}; a low cap concentrates the loss on the last \textit{c} cycles, where the alarm is raised.

\textbf{Trigger.} The estimate is smoothed causally and shifted, $\tilde{y}$\textsubscript{t} = EWM\textsubscript{m}($\hat{y}$)\textsubscript{t} + \textit{s}, with span \textit{m} $\in$ \{1, 3, 6\} (\textit{m} = 1: no smoothing) and \textit{s} $\in$ \{\textminus{}12, \textminus{}11.5, \ldots{}, +8\}, and the alarm is raised at

\begin{equation}
t^{*} = \min\{\, t : \tilde{y}_{\tau} \le H \ \text{for all}\ \tau = t-k+1, \ldots, t \,\},
\end{equation}

with debounce \textit{k} $\in$ \{1, 2, 3, 5\}; the reported estimate is $\tilde{y}$\textsubscript{t*}.

\textbf{Nested selection.} The training units of each outer fold are split into five inner folds. Regressors fitted on four inner folds, which also supply the normalisation statistics, predict every cycle of the fifth, giving out-of-fold predictions for all training units. For each \textit{H}, the grid (caps with \textit{c} $\ge$ \textit{H} + 20, three spans, four debounce values and 41 shifts) is searched for the setting with the most correct out-of-fold first warnings; ties go to the smaller |\textit{s}|. The regressor is then refitted on all training units, $\theta$ is frozen, and each test unit is processed once. Supplementary Section S13 lists every setting.

\subsection{The role of the shift}

Let $\varepsilon$ = EWM\textsubscript{m}($\hat{y}$)\textsubscript{t*} \textminus{} \textit{r}\textsubscript{t*} be the unshifted error at the alarm, so that \textit{e} = $\varepsilon$ + \textit{s}, and write the window as [\textit{a}, \textit{b}] = [\textminus{}13, +2], of width \textit{w} = 15. The following elementary result explains why a negative shift helps. It is stated for a \textbf{fixed} error distribution.

\textbf{Proposition 1.} Let $\varepsilon$ have a continuous distribution \textit{F} that does not depend on \textit{s} (assumption A1). Then the probability that \textit{e} = $\varepsilon$ + \textit{s} lies in [\textit{a}, \textit{b}] is

\begin{equation}
P(s) = F(b - s) - F(a - s).
\end{equation}

(i) \textit{P}(\textit{s}) $\le$ \textit{C}\textsubscript{w}(\textit{F}) = sup\textsubscript{x} [\textit{F}(\textit{x} + \textit{w}) \textminus{} \textit{F}(\textit{x})] for every \textit{s}. (ii) If \textit{F} has a density that is unimodal and symmetric about its median \textit{m}, \textit{P}(\textit{s}) is maximised at

\begin{equation}
s^{*} = (a + b)/2 - m = -5.5 - m,
\end{equation}

with maximum Pr(|$\varepsilon$ \textminus{} \textit{m}| $\le$ 7.5). The proof is in Supplementary Section S2.

The result implies that an unbiased estimator should be shifted by about \textminus{}5.5 cycles, because the window is centred at \textminus{}5.5. It is \textbf{not} a guarantee for the sequential policy: changing \textit{s} changes the stopping time \textit{t}\textsuperscript{*} and therefore the distribution of $\varepsilon$, so in practice \textit{F} depends on \textit{s}. Section 6.2 measures how far the alarm moves when the shift changes and how far the realised rate departs from \textit{P}(\textit{s}).

\subsection{Time scale of the battery and bearing data}

The protocols aggregated consecutive cycles or snapshots into blocks of $\kappa$ = max(1, round(median life / 209.5)), 209.5 cycles being the median PHM08 life, so that the grids designed for engine lives could be reused unchanged. As executed under the protocols, the median was taken over all units of a dataset, including those later used as test units, so held-out lives could influence the time scale; for the batteries the all-cell median (731 cycles) lies just below the boundary between $\kappa$ = 3 and $\kappa$ = 4 (733.25 cycles). We report those protocol results for provenance and base every interpretation on training-only time scales. Three conventions appear below: \textbf{A}, $\kappa$ = 3 from all cells, as executed under the protocol (provenance only); \textbf{B}, $\kappa$ from the outer training cells, inherited by the inner folds, with the task in blocks; and \textbf{C}, fully nested preprocessing with the task in cycles, described next. Convention C is the only one in which no held-out life enters any time scale or setting, and it is the primary convention for every battery result unless stated otherwise. The history of the correction, including an intermediate analysis in which inner folds inherited the outer training set's $\kappa$, is in the correction note (Supplementary Section S16).

\textbf{Fully nested preprocessing (batteries).} The battery task is defined in physical units (Section 3.4). Within each outer training set, every inner training subset builds its own blocks, its own z-score statistics and its own regressor, with $\kappa$ computed from that subset only. Its inner validation cells are scored in their own blocks against the same physical window, so the out-of-fold counts of the five inner folds can be summed per setting even when their $\kappa$ differ. After selection, blocks, statistics and regressor are rebuilt from the complete outer training set, with $\kappa$ from that set, and the outer test cells are scored once. This is done under cross-validation by cell (five folds, seed 2026) and with each of the three batches held out in turn. Three ways of choosing $\kappa$ are reported:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{rule, nested}: $\kappa$ by the protocol rule computed from each training subset (inner subsets for selection, the outer training set for the final model);
\item \textbf{selected by inner cross-validation}: $\kappa$ $\in$ \{2, \ldots{}, 6\} with the largest summed out-of-fold count (ties to the outer rule value, then to the smaller $\kappa$);
\item \textbf{fixed}: each $\kappa$ $\in$ \{2, \ldots{}, 6\} in turn, as a sensitivity analysis.
\end{itemize}

No failure life outside a training subset enters its time scale or its selection, so neither the outer test estimates nor the inner selections use held-out lives. \textbf{Bearings.} The bearing analyses use $\kappa$ from the outer training set (inner folds inherit it), and a sensitivity analysis holds the physical task fixed for $\kappa$ $\in$ \{3, 5, 7, 10, 14\}. Inheriting $\kappa$ can influence which settings are selected, but not the outer test estimate. We did not repeat the bearing analysis with full nesting, because every variant run gave at most one correct alarm in 17. The battery cost analysis (Tables 8 and 10) is fully nested in the same way, with the task, the lead times and every threshold in cycles; an earlier version that took $\kappa$ from the outer training set and expressed the window-tuned task in blocks is kept in Supplementary Section S11 for provenance. The battery timing controls and shift study (Section 6.2) still take $\kappa$ from the outer training cells, with the task in blocks.

\textbf{How an operator would choose $\kappa$.} In service, lives are unknown, but three sources of information are not: the lives of units previously run to failure (the training fleet, as here), design or warranty lives, and the notice that a replacement needs. We therefore regard the task as fixed in physical units and $\kappa$ as a temporal resolution, chosen from historical lives or by cross-validation on them.

\subsection{Comparisons}

\textbf{Fixed-time versus self-timed (PHM08).} Eleven fixed-time models (Supplementary Section S1) are evaluated under a fit-75\%/calibrate-25\% protocol: trained on 75\% of the training engines of each outer fold, calibrated where applicable on the other 25\%, and scored at 30, 50, 70 and 90\% of each test engine's life. Because the recipe is fitted on all outer training engines, that comparison confounds the training allocation with everything else. The matched comparison therefore uses one cross-fitting procedure for every variant. Inner five-fold out-of-fold predictions of the outer training engines (seed 100 + fold, normalisation from each inner training subset) are used for every calibration or selection step, and the final model is refitted on all outer training engines. Outer test engines never enter fitting, calibration or selection. The variants change one element at a time. The \textbf{observation time} is 50\% or 90\% of life, the cycle with exactly 20 true cycles left (an oracle time that cannot be deployed), or the policy's own alarm. The \textbf{regressor} is either the fixed-time gradient-boosted model (no target cap, features of seven temperature and speed sensors) or the recipe's capped regressor. \textbf{Calibration} is a shift chosen on the out-of-fold errors at the same observation time; the trigger variants choose span, debounce and shift on out-of-fold alarms. A matched 75\%/25\% comparison, in which both the fixed-time model and the recipe are fitted on the same 75\% and calibrated or tuned on the same 25\%, is reported as well. The cross-fitted variants were repeated on FD004, which has two fault modes and six regimes, with the same seeds.

\textbf{Identical-protocol competitors (PHM08).} In the spirit of the three winning PHM08 approaches we re-implemented a similarity method \cite{wang2008}, an LSTM \cite{heimes2008,zheng2017} and a multilayer perceptron followed by a scalar Kalman filter \cite{peel2008}, and added a convolutional network \cite{li2018}. All were run through the same trigger, each with its warning settings chosen on calibration engines (25\% of each outer training part; seed 7) and the shift grid \textminus{}15 to +10. An LSTM, a CNN and a hybrid Transformer were also given exactly the recipe's inputs and cap set. The re-implementations are not the original code, and only the networks' caps and warning settings were tuned.

\textbf{Timing controls.} A \textit{constant-estimate control} keeps the recipe's alarm times and replaces the reported estimate by one constant per fold, chosen on training out-of-fold alarms; it can show only whether the estimate adds information beyond the alarm time. A \textit{health-indicator threshold alarm} has no remaining-life regressor: a linear health indicator, in the spirit of Wang et al. \cite{wang2008}, is fitted to separate the first 30 from the last five cycles of each training unit, and the alarm is raised when its smoothed value stays above a threshold for \textit{k} cycles, reporting a constant in [\textit{H} \textminus{} 6, \textit{H}]. A \textit{calibrated health-indicator alarm} tests whether a fairer indicator-based opponent closes the gap: the same linear indicator is mapped to cycles to failure by an isotonic, decreasing regression of min(\textit{r}, \textit{c}) on its inner out-of-fold values (\textit{c} $\in$ \{40, 60, 90\}), and the mapped series goes through the recipe's trigger with the recipe's grids and selection rule.

\textbf{Battery baselines.} Two baselines use the measured discharge capacity directly: a threshold alarm on smoothed block-mean capacity that reports a constant, and a capacity trend that extrapolates a least-squares line through the capacity of the last 30, 60 or 120 cycles to the end-of-life capacity of 0.88 Ah and feeds the extrapolated remaining life to the same trigger. Neither fits a model, so their settings are chosen on the training cells themselves. They are evaluated on the window, on the notice they give and on cost. \textbf{Ablation (PHM08).} Each variant changes one component of the recipe and re-tunes the rest.

\subsection{Statistics and what the uncertainty covers}

Proportions carry Clopper--Pearson 95\% intervals \cite{clopper1934}. Paired comparisons of in-window rates use the exact McNemar test \cite{mcnemar1947}. Holm's correction \cite{holm1979} is applied over the seven competitors at each level of the identical-protocol comparison, over the nine ablation variants, and, for the health-indicator alarm, over the seven datasets of the timing-control table (six engine datasets for its calibrated version). The constant-estimate comparisons are reported unadjusted, since none was significant even before adjustment. The pre-specified hypothesis tests are reported unadjusted, as fixed in the protocols. A non-significant difference is not read as equivalence, because no equivalence margin was specified. Ratios of cost rates carry paired 95\% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of test units with the fitted policies held fixed. The 16 lead-time and cost-ratio cells of a dataset share the same units and alarms, so they are not independent replications; we report the range of ratios over cells and, descriptively, the number of cells whose interval excludes 1.

These intervals cover the sampling of test units given the fitted policies. They omit the variability from refitting the regressor, from the assignment of units to folds and from the selection of settings. Three results indicate its size. On PHM08, thirty repeated random 50-engine test sets with settings re-selected each time gave a standard deviation of 3.3 percentage points (Supplementary Section S9). On the batteries, changing the time scale ($\kappa$ = 2 to 6) moved the cross-validation rate by up to 3.3 points and the held-out-batch rate by up to 21.1 points. A change of the filling of one missing cycle moved per-split counts by up to three cells (Supplementary Section S5). Four leakage checks were run on PHM08: disjoint outer and inner units, causality of the features, a negative control with permuted lives, and exact reproduction of a refitted fold (Supplementary Section S4); the same four were run on every dataset of the comparison with the metric \textit{M} (Supplementary Section S17). For that comparison, thirty random 80/20 splits of FD001 show the spread of an evaluation on 20 test units; because they overlap, they are summarised by their distribution, not by a pooled interval.

Table 5 and Fig. 2 separate the contributions on PHM08. \textbf{Observation time} dominates. Scored at half of life, where the change of a pooled health signal from its early-life level (median 0.23) is comparable to its cycle-to-cycle noise (0.21), the calibrated fixed-time model placed 24.8\% of estimates in the window. Under the fit-75\%/calibrate-25\% protocol, the best of eleven fixed-time models at half of life, a stacked hybrid whose quantile stacker and shift were learned on the calibration engines, placed 33.5\% (Fig. 2a shows three of them; Supplementary Section S1 lists all eleven). At 90\% of life (median 21 cycles left; at 20 cycles left the health signal has risen to about 1.08) the same model placed 85.3\%. \textbf{Training allocation} mattered less: fitting on 75\% of the training engines and calibrating on the other 25\% gave 81.2\% for the fixed-time model and 89.4\% for the recipe, against 85.3\% and 91.7\% with cross-fitting on all of them, and neither difference was significant.

\textbf{Target and features} made a difference at a fixed observation time. At 90\% of life the recipe's capped regressor placed 91.7\% in the window against 85.3\% for the fixed-time model (27 against 13 discordant engines, \textit{p} = 0.038). At exactly 20 cycles left the figures were 89.9\% against 84.4\% (27 against 15 discordant engines, \textit{p} = 0.088). \textbf{Self-timing} did not add measurable accuracy for either regressor. With the trigger, the fixed-time model reached 84.4\%, its rate at 20 cycles left (17 against 17 discordant engines). The recipe's self-timed first warning reached 91.7\%, the same rate as its regressor scored at 90\% of life (8 against 8 discordant engines, \textit{p} = 1.000). Against the calibrated fixed-time model at 90\% of life the self-timed recipe was higher (26 against 12 discordant engines, \textit{p} = 0.034), but that difference is accounted for by the regressor, not by the timing.

\textbf{A second dataset.} The cross-fitted variants were repeated on FD004 (Table 5, last column), whose 249 engines have two fault modes and six regimes. The calibrated fixed-time model placed 18.9\% of its estimates in the window at half of life and 66.7\% at 90\% of life. Target and features mattered more than on PHM08: at 90\% of life the recipe's regressor placed 82.3\% (61 against 22 discordant engines, \textit{p} < 0.001). The self-timed recipe placed 82.7\%, no more than its regressor at 90\% of life (18 against 17 discordant engines, \textit{p} = 1.000); with the trigger, the fixed-time model reached 72.3\%. By the criterion fixed before this analysis (at least 30 points between half and 90\% of life, and self-timing not significantly above the same regressor at 90\% of life), the PHM08 conclusion repeats on FD004. One difference from PHM08 is that the regressor scored at the oracle time of 20 cycles left was more accurate than the self-timed alarm on FD004 (89.2\% against 82.7\%; correct only at the oracle time 32 engines, only with the alarm 16, \textit{p} = 0.029), so with two fault modes self-timing lost some accuracy relative to an observation time that cannot be known in service.

The answer to Q1 is therefore that the contrast between long-range fixed-time prediction (24.8\% at half of life under cross-fitting; 33.5\% for the best of eleven models under the fit-75\%/calibrate-25\% protocol, against 89.4\% for the recipe under that protocol) and the self-timed alarm (91.7\%) on PHM08 is mostly a contrast of observation time, with a further, smaller contribution from target and feature design, which was larger on FD004. What self-timing contributes is not accuracy but the ability to act without knowing the life: a fraction of life cannot be observed in service, whereas the alarm reaches near-failure accuracy from the history alone, with no missed engine and a median of 19 cycles of notice. Whether such an alarm is a good maintenance policy is a separate question. On a grid of decision times, for example, it must be mapped to the next decision time, which raised its metric \textit{M} on FD001 from 3.93\% to 8.08\% (Section 7). These are paired comparisons on 218 and 249 engines of one simulator family, and differences of a few points could go undetected.

\textbf{Timing, not the reported number.} Because the alarm is raised when the smoothed, shifted estimate first stays below \textit{H}, the reported estimate varies little between units: on PHM08 at \textit{H} = 20 its standard deviation at the alarm was 2.1 cycles (5--95\%: 11.2--18.2), against 4.8 cycles for the true remaining life. Correctness, $\hat{y}$ \textminus{} 2 $\le$ \textit{r} $\le$ $\hat{y}$ + 13, therefore depends mainly on where the alarm falls. For $\hat{y}$ between 11.2 and 18.2 the accepted range of \textit{r} runs from at least 9.2 to at most 31.2 cycles; a fixed band is only an approximation of this condition. In Table 7, the recipe's alarm times with one constant estimate per fold placed 90.8\% of first warnings in the window over the five engine datasets, against 90.2\% with the model's estimate (27 against 33 discordant engines, \textit{p} = 0.519). Per dataset the constant scored between \textminus{}2.4 and +3.6 percentage points relative to the model's estimate: lower on FD002, FD003, Battery, higher on FD001, FD004, N-CMAPSS, equal on PHM08. No difference was significant, but no equivalence margin was tested. Because this control keeps the recipe's alarm times, it shows only that the reported estimate added no detectable information beyond the alarm time; it says nothing about how good those times are.

\textbf{How good the times are} is shown by the health-indicator threshold alarm, which times its alarm on a classical linear health indicator. It placed 49.4--57.0\% of first warnings in the window, significantly fewer than the recipe on each of the seven datasets after Holm's correction (largest adjusted \textit{p} = 0.020, N-CMAPSS), with many alarms too early (64 of 218 on PHM08). A fairer indicator-based opponent maps the same indicator to cycles to failure and uses the recipe's own trigger and selection rule. On PHM08 and FD001--FD004 this calibrated indicator placed 48.0--55.8\% of first warnings in the window, close to the uncalibrated alarm and significantly fewer than the recipe on each (largest Holm-adjusted \textit{p} < 0.001). On N-CMAPSS it placed 69.7\%, against 68.7\% for the recipe (19 against 20 discordant engines, Holm-adjusted \textit{p} = 1.000) and 53.5\% for the uncalibrated alarm. By the interpretation fixed before this analysis, on PHM08 and FD001--FD004 the gradient-boosted regressor contributes timing information that a linear indicator expressed in cycles does not, whereas on N-CMAPSS, where the recipe itself was weak, calibration in cycles accounts for the recipe's timing.

\textbf{What the score conceals.} A correct alarm with \textit{H} = 20 means a remaining life of roughly 9 to 31 cycles; the score does not distinguish 10 from 30 cycles of notice, nor say whether the notice suffices for a given lead time. It treats every error inside [\textminus{}13, +2] alike and every error outside it alike, whatever its size, and it is silent about cost. Section 6.4 shows, on the battery cells, a case where the policy with the higher window score gave much less notice, and Section 6.3 shows that window tuning and cost tuning ranked differently by cost on different datasets.

\textbf{The shift moves the alarm.} Proposition 1 assumes that the error distribution does not change with the shift. We varied the shift on the test units of each fold by up to $\pm$4 around the value selected on training data at \textit{H} = 20, keeping the other settings, and recorded the alarm cycle, the true remaining life at the alarm and the unshifted error $\varepsilon$ (Fig. 6). On each of the seven datasets the shift acted mainly by moving the alarm. Each unit of shift changed the median true remaining life at the alarm by \textminus{}1.0 to \textminus{}0.9 cycles (blocks for the battery), against \textminus{}0.1 to 0.1 for the median of $\varepsilon$. Assumption A1 is therefore violated by construction, yet the error at the alarm changed slowly. Within $\pm$2 of the selected shift, the largest difference between the realised rate and the rate predicted by Proposition 1 from the errors at the selected shift was 1.6 to 8.0 percentage points, depending on the dataset (largest on FD001); within $\pm$4 it was 4.2 to 8.0 points. The proposition thus describes the direction and rough size of the response to the shift, with errors of several points. It is not a bound for the sequential policy and says nothing about what other features or models could achieve. A low rate is therefore not evidence that no shift could do better, nor is the concentration of the out-of-fold error a deployment check.

\textbf{Bearings.} The recipe failed. None of the 17 first warnings fell in the window, either in the protocol analysis or with $\kappa$ from the training bearings (7 too early and 10 missed). The competitors were also almost always wrong (age-only 1, MLP with Kalman filter 0 of 17). With the physical task fixed (\textit{H} = 140 snapshots), block lengths of 3, 5, 7, 10, 14 snapshots gave at most 1 of 17. The pre-specified fifth hypothesis, that STW-T would be no more than 5 points worse, was met only trivially (0\% against 0\%), which cannot establish non-inferiority. A post hoc diagnosis with an ad hoc criterion (Supplementary Section S7) found that in 11 of 17 bearings the vibration level rose to and stayed above 1.5 times its early-life level only within the last six blocks before failure (about seven minutes), or never. 6 of these were never warned, which is consistent with failure becoming visible too late for a 20-block alarm. The other 5 received alarms 63--211 blocks before failure, long before any sustained rise, which late visibility cannot explain. Of the 6 bearings with longer degradation, 4 were missed and 2 warned too early. Late onset is therefore at most a partial explanation; small training sets (13--14 bearings per fold), three operating conditions and the feature set are competing explanations that were not tested. The result shows that this recipe, with these features, did not work on these bearings, not that advance warning of bearing failure is impossible.

\section{Relation to the metric \textit{M} of Kamariotis et al.}\label{sec:7}

Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024} assess a prognostic algorithm through the maintenance decisions that a policy takes with its predictions. Decisions can be taken only at times \textit{t}\textsubscript{k} = \textit{k}$\Delta$\textit{T}; at each one the policy replaces the component preventively, at cost \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}, or lets it run; a component that fails first is replaced correctively at cost \textit{c}\textsubscript{c} > \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}. With \textit{C}\textsubscript{i} the cost and \textit{T}\textsubscript{i} the length of the life cycle of test unit \textit{i}, the long-run cost rate is estimated by \textit{R} = $\Sigma$\textit{C}\textsubscript{i} / $\Sigma$\textit{T}\textsubscript{i}, and the metric is its relative excess over a perfect policy that replaces every unit at the last decision time before its failure,

\begin{equation}
M = \bigl( R - R_{\mathrm{perf}} \bigr) / R_{\mathrm{perf}}.
\end{equation}

They use \textit{M} to compare algorithms, to choose policy thresholds on training units and to tune hyperparameters, on FD001 split into 80 training and 20 test engines with $\Delta$\textit{T} = 10 cycles, and they state that an evaluation of \textit{M} on 20 units is subject to significant statistical uncertainty. We re-implemented the metric, including its first-order variance (their Eqs. 2--9), and evaluated our alarms and their policies on our regressor under their conventions. Their code and splits are not available to us, so this is a re-implementation, not a reproduction; we do not compare with the values they report, which come from other models on one 20-engine split. Everything in this section is exploratory; the analysis plans, including seeds and the definition of a win, were hashed before the runs (Table 3).

\subsection{Conversion and differences of convention}

With $\ell$ = 0 and $\rho$ = \textit{c}\textsubscript{c}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{p}, our cost rate relative to perfect foresight (Section 3.3) and \textit{M} differ only in the reference policy. Ours replaces at \textit{L} \textminus{} 1; theirs at the last decision time before \textit{L}. Hence the ratio of Section 3.3 equals (1 + \textit{M})(1 + \textit{g}), where \textit{g} = $\Sigma$(\textit{L}\textsubscript{i} \textminus{} 1) / $\Sigma$\textit{T}\textsubscript{i}\textsuperscript{perf} \textminus{} 1 is a property of the test lives and the grid alone. The ratio equals 1 + \textit{M} only for $\Delta$\textit{T} = 1. With $\Delta$\textit{T} = 10, \textit{g} was 1.7--2.3\% on PHM08 and C-MAPSS and 7.3\% on N-CMAPSS, whose lives of 48--100 flight cycles make a 10-cycle grid coarse; we therefore report no \textit{M} for N-CMAPSS. Three further differences matter. (1) \textbf{Decision grid.} Our alarms can fire at any cycle. On their grid an alarm acts at the next decision time, and an alarm whose decision time is at or beyond the failure counts as a failure. Without this mapping \textit{M} can be negative: in the earlier battery cost analysis (convention B) with $\Delta$\textit{T} = 30 cycles, the unmapped capacity-threshold policy reached \textit{M} = \textminus{}0.37\%, because its alarms fell between decision times. Our policies were therefore re-selected on training units under the grid-mapped cost. (2) \textbf{Lead time.} Their replacement-only setting has no lead time, so the comparison uses $\ell$ = 0 only; their ordering setting, which has a different lead-time model, was not implemented. (3) \textbf{Cost ratios.} We use \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} $\in$ \{0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8\}, chosen to span the ranges shown in their figures; 0.1, the only ratio for which their text reports a value, is primary and the others are descriptive.

\subsection{Their policies on our regressor}

Their policy 1 replaces at the first decision time at which Pr(RUL $\le$ $\Delta$\textit{T}) $\ge$ \textit{p}\textsubscript{thres}, with \textit{p}\textsubscript{thres} = \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} or a threshold optimised on training units; their policy 3 replaces when the replacement time that minimises the expected cost under the predictive RUL distribution, with the remaining life valued at an age-replacement cost rate, falls before the next decision time (their Eq. 14, option 1). Both need a failure probability, which our point regressor does not give. We built it in two ways from training units only: \textbf{P-cls}, a gradient-boosted classifier of RUL $\le$ 10 on the recipe's features, calibrated by isotonic regression on inner out-of-fold predictions; and \textbf{P-res}, the empirical distribution of the true RUL in equal-count bins of the inner out-of-fold estimates of the cap-60 regressor (at least 200 rows per bin, monotonicity enforced, RUL above the cap set to the cap; cap 125 as a sensitivity analysis for policy 3). Differences between their policies and ours therefore reflect the probability model as much as the policy. Optimised thresholds were chosen on the inner out-of-fold predictions; when several thresholds tied, the middle of the tied range was used, and the largest tied threshold, which corresponds to the choice shown in their Fig. 9a, was computed as well. A policy is said to beat another only when the paired 95\% bootstrap interval of the difference in \textit{M} (2,000 resamples of test units, the grid-perfect reference recomputed in each) excludes zero in the five-fold run, which tests each unit once. On their simulator (their Section 3) our implementation reproduced their qualitative findings at all seven ratios: the optimised policy 1 had the lowest \textit{M} and policy 3 was below policy 2. There, as in their study, the threshold was optimised and scored on the same simulated units, so that agreement partly reflects in-sample optimisation.

It did so at 5 of the seven ratios against the window-tuned alarm and 6 against the 1-SE alarm, and ours never beat it. Our alarms beat their policy 3 (10.34\%) and age replacement (79.25\%). Against policy 1 with P-cls (12.02\%, 1 failure) the comparison was unclear at every ratio: one failure among 100 units widens the interval to tens of points. Two observations explain the result. First, the grid penalises an alarm that was not designed for it: our window-tuned alarm, scored at its own cycle, reached 3.93\%, but such an alarm is not admissible on their grid, and mapped to it, with settings re-selected for the grid, it reached 8.08\%. Second, with bins about 10 cycles wide near failure, P-res policy 1 amounts to replacing at the first decision time at which the regressor's estimate falls below about 21.5 cycles: the same regressor, used through a simple grid-aware rule, gave lower \textit{M} than through our trigger. The interval of their Eq. 7 treats \textit{R}\textsubscript{perf} as fixed. It was $\pm$4.58 points even for the perfect policy, whose \textit{M} is 0 by definition, which is why our verdicts use the paired bootstrap.

\textbf{FD002--FD004 and the spread over splits.} At the primary ratio, policy 1 with P-res beat the window-tuned alarm on FD001, the difference was unclear on FD004, and the window-tuned alarm beat it on FD002 and FD003. Against the 1-SE alarm it won on FD001 and FD004, was unclear on FD003 and lost on FD002. Our window-tuned alarm beat policy 3 on FD001, FD002, FD003 and FD004 and age replacement on all four datasets. Against policy 1 with P-cls it never won, lost on FD003 and was otherwise unclear. The lowest \textit{M} at this ratio was reached by their policy 1 (P-res) on FD001 (5.69\%), our window-tuned alarm on FD002 (4.85\%), their policy 1 (P-cls) on FD003 (3.46\%) and our window-tuned alarm on FD004 (8.44\%) (Table 12), so neither family of policies was best everywhere, and on FD004 no difference between our window-tuned alarm and their policy 1 was clear. On thirty random 80/20 splits of FD001, which overlap and are therefore summarised without a pooled interval (Supplementary Section S17), the median \textit{M} at 0.1 was 3.62\% for policy 1 with P-cls, 5.88\% with P-res, 5.32\% for the 1-SE alarm and 8.92\% for the window-tuned alarm. A failure among the 20 test engines occurred in 3, 1, 3 and 3 of the 30 splits, respectively, and raised \textit{M} in such a split to as much as 92.4\%, which illustrates the uncertainty of a single 20-unit evaluation that Kamariotis et al. point out.

\subsection{Robustness of direct threshold optimisation}

Their Fig. 9a shows the risk of optimising \textit{p}\textsubscript{thres} when failures are expensive: the training optimum lies at the edge of a plateau, and a slightly larger threshold lets a unit or two fail and makes \textit{M} very large. The same shape appeared on our data (Fig. 10b; FD001, fold 1, \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} = 0.01, P-cls, chosen before the run). Every threshold from 0.026 to 0.032 gave the minimal training \textit{M} of 3.02\%; the next threshold, 0.0328, let 1 of the 80 training engines fail and raised the training \textit{M} to 129.9\%. With P-res the step was larger (11 training failures and \textit{M} = 1362.4\% just above the plateau), because its probabilities take only a few values. The largest tied threshold, the choice their Fig. 9a illustrates, therefore sat one grid step from the training cliff; the middle of the tie sat further from it. On the test engines of that fold neither choice produced a failure.

Across test sets the risk materialised unevenly (Fig. 10c). At \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} $\le$ 0.1, the five folds of FD001--FD004 give 80 fold--ratio cells for each policy. Policy 1 with an optimised threshold had at least one test failure in 12 of them with P-cls and 4 with P-res, against 19 and 11 with \textit{p}\textsubscript{thres} = \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c}; plain cost tuning of our alarm had a failure in 24, the one-standard-error rule in 4 and window tuning in 0. Over the thirty random FD001 splits the corresponding counts, of 120 split--ratio cells, were 12 and 4 for optimised policy 1, 20 and 4 with \textit{p}\textsubscript{thres} = \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c}, 44 for plain cost tuning, 12 with the one-standard-error rule and 12 for window tuning. In 62\% of the five-fold cells with a failure, that failure raised \textit{M} above 50\%. By the decision rule fixed before the run (each plainly optimised policy must fail in more cells than its regularised or heuristic counterpart), the claim that direct cost tuning is fragile was weakened on all four datasets and over the thirty splits. Plain cost tuning of our alarms was clearly more fragile than the one-standard-error rule; for policy 1 the comparison went the other way: with \textit{p}\textsubscript{thres} = \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} it had a test failure in 19 cells with P-cls and 11 with P-res, against 12 and 4 with optimised thresholds. Window-tuned alarms had a failure in 0 of the 80 five-fold cells but in 12 of the 120 split cells, so the margin that window tuning builds in did not always hold. Measured in cycles, the plainly tuned threshold lay a median of 5.5 cycles above the largest threshold at which a test unit failed, against 9.5 cycles with the one-standard-error rule. The thresholds that minimised the training cost lay below \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} at the larger ratios (median 0.028 for P-cls on FD001 at \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} = 0.1), as in their simulations, so the heuristic replaced later there. The fragility therefore concerns plain minimisation of an empirical cost over many settings, which a one-standard-error rule mitigated, more than the optimisation of a single probability threshold. The middle and the largest tied thresholds gave different test decisions in 1 of 9,926 cases on FD001--FD004 (largest difference in \textit{M} 0.2 points) and in none of 8,400 over the thirty FD001 splits, so the tie rule did not drive these results.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:8}

\subsection{Answers to the four questions}

\textbf{Q1.} The four quantities behaved differently, and under matched training data and cross-fitted calibration the contrast between fixed-time and self-timed evaluation came mostly from the observation time. On PHM08 the same calibrated fixed-time model placed 24.8\% of its estimates in the window at half of life and 85.3\% at 90\% of life, and on FD004 18.9\% and 66.7\%. The recipe's capped regressor added about 6.4 points at 90\% of life on PHM08 (\textit{p} = 0.038), and self-timing added nothing measurable over the same regressor at 90\% of life on either dataset, although on FD004 it fell short of the regressor at the oracle time of 20 cycles left. Long-range accuracy therefore says little about near-failure accuracy, and near-failure accuracy says little about the timing of a self-timed alarm or about maintenance cost. The practical value of self-timing is that it reaches near-failure accuracy without knowing the life; it makes the alarm operable, not more accurate, and Section 7 shows that operable is not the same as cost-efficient on a decision grid.

\textbf{Q2.} The asymmetric window rewards alarm timing. Because the alarm fires when the smoothed estimate first crosses a fixed level, the reported estimate is nearly constant at the alarm; a constant estimate at the same alarm times scored within 3.6 points of the model's estimate on each of the seven datasets on which it was run (no equivalence margin was tested). A health-indicator threshold alarm scored significantly lower on all seven, and calibrating the indicator to cycles and giving it the recipe's trigger did not close the gap on PHM08 and FD001--FD004; on N-CMAPSS it matched the recipe. Good timing therefore came from the regressor's features and model on the datasets where the recipe was accurate. The score conceals how much notice the alarm gives and what the alarms cost. On the battery cells, the capacity trend had the higher window score within batches but gave a correct alarm with at least 45 cycles left on 32.5\% of cells, against 80.5\% for the recipe. A window score should therefore be reported together with the distribution of the remaining life at the alarm, or the share of correct alarms with the notice that the maintenance task needs.

\textbf{Q3.} Window tuning gave useful policies where the alarms were accurate and the test units resembled the training units: window-tuned alarms were significantly cheaper than optimal age replacement in most cost settings on the five C-MAPSS datasets and on the battery cells, but not on N-CMAPSS. Direct cost optimisation was better on some datasets and worse on others (Table 8), and no tuning rule was cheapest on every dataset. Under the metric \textit{M} of Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024}, the ranking of our alarms and their policy 1 built on the same regressor depended on the dataset and on the probability model: at the primary cost ratio their policy 1 with a residual-based failure probability beat our window-tuned alarm on FD001 and lost to it on FD002 and FD003, and with a classifier-based probability it beat it on FD003; the lowest \textit{M} came from their policy 1 (P-res) on FD001 (5.69\%), our window-tuned alarm on FD002 (4.85\%), their policy 1 (P-cls) on FD003 (3.46\%) and our window-tuned alarm on FD004 (8.44\%). Our alarms beat their policy 3 on FD001, FD002, FD003 and FD004 and age replacement everywhere. Window tuning is therefore not a reliable surrogate for cost optimisation, but under their conventions it was not systematically worse than their simple policy either.

\textbf{Q4.} The recipe's results from familiar conditions did not carry over to new ones. On the battery cells, under a common physical task with fully nested preprocessing, the in-window rate fell from 95.1\% under cross-validation by cell to 79.7\% with a whole batch held out. Across the $\kappa$ conventions examined, the held-out-batch rate lay between 63.4\% and 84.6\%, below every within-batch estimate and below the protocol criterion. The capacity trend, in contrast, went from 100.0\% to 98.4\%, and it was also the cheaper signal in most cost settings. On N-CMAPSS, the same simulator family under different operating profiles, the recipe reached 68.7\%, and on the bearings, a different asset type, none of 17 alarms was correct. Within-batch cross-validation is therefore an upper reference, not an estimate for a new campaign. Each new population needs its own validation, with whole batches or campaigns held out and with a simple physically motivated baseline where one exists.

\subsection{Window tuning, cost tuning and the metric M}

Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024} argue that prognostic algorithms and the policies built on them should be judged and tuned by the long-run cost of the decisions they induce. Our results support that view, with two qualifications. The first concerns how the cost is minimised. Minimising the empirical cost rate over about two thousand settings sometimes selected alarms with too little margin and produced large losses when failures were expensive. A one-standard-error rule reduced the largest losses, a lower quantile was often cheaper but did not avoid them, and window tuning was cheaper than the one-standard-error rule in most cells only on FD003. The window is best regarded as an operational specification, not a proxy for cost.

The second qualification concerns the decision rule. With the same regressor, a threshold on the probability of failure before the next decision time gave lower \textit{M} than our self-timed trigger on FD001, and higher \textit{M} on FD002 and FD003. Where our trigger lost, two of its features explain much of the difference: it was chosen for accuracy on the window, not for the notice that the decision grid requires, and it fires between decision times, so that on a grid it acts later than intended or must warn earlier to avoid failures. Where it won, it avoided failures: across the five folds of the four datasets the window-tuned alarm had no test failure at any \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} $\le$ 0.1, although over the thirty random FD001 splits it had at least one in 12 of 120 split--ratio cells (optimised policy 1: 12 with P-cls, 4 with P-res). The narrow late side of the window plausibly builds in that margin, but the mechanism was not tested. Against their policy 1 with the classifier-based probability the comparison was unclear on FD001, FD002 and FD004, so the result depends on how the failure probability is built as well as on the policy. Plain minimisation of the empirical cost was fragile in the way their Fig. 9a describes: the largest tied training optimum sat one threshold step from a cliff for both probability models, and plainly cost-tuned alarms failed on test units in 24 of 80 fold--ratio cells at \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} $\le$ 0.1, against 4 with the one-standard-error rule. For their policy 1, however, the heuristic threshold \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} was no safer than the optimised one. Neither rule is therefore a safe default: where cost ratios, lead times and decision times can be stated, a policy that uses them explicitly, with a safeguard against selection noise, should be compared with window tuning on the fleet's own history.

The battery results add a further qualification: on these cells the choice of signal mattered more than the choice of tuning criterion. Window-tuned and cost-tuned versions of the capacity trend had a lower cost rate than the recipe policy with which they were paired in every cost setting under cross-validation, and they were never significantly more expensive with a batch held out (Table 10). Comparing tuning criteria for one regressor is therefore no substitute for comparing the regressor with a simple baseline aimed at the failure definition.

\subsection{Practical implications and relation to earlier work}

The following implications hold under the assumptions evaluated here: simulated or laboratory run-to-failure data, a fixed lead time, independent units and replacement as good as new. (i) Long-range prediction, near-failure estimation, alarm timing and maintenance cost should be evaluated separately, because good performance on one did not imply good performance on another. (ii) An alarm window used as an acceptance criterion constrains when alarms are raised. It should be accompanied by the notice actually given and, where possible, by the cost of the alarms under the decision times that will be used in service. (iii) For the recipe, validation within familiar test conditions overstated performance on a held-out batch by 14 to 32 percentage points, depending on the time-scale convention. Held-out campaigns, batches or operating conditions are the more relevant check. (iv) Where failure is defined by a threshold on a measured quantity, a simple extrapolation of that quantity is a necessary baseline. (v) When a cost metric such as \textit{M} is reported, the decision grid, the perfect reference and the way alarms are mapped to decision times should be stated, because they change its value by several percentage points.

These findings relate to the two closest studies as follows. de Pater et al. \cite{depater2022} trigger maintenance scheduling from alarms on probabilistic prognostics and evaluate the resulting fleet cost. Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024} score prognostic algorithms by the increase in long-run maintenance cost they induce relative to perfect prognostics, and tune heuristic policies and algorithm hyperparameters to that metric. Our results support their emphasis on decision cost: an operational window score did not reliably rank policies by cost, and a policy with a higher window score could give less notice. Re-implementing their metric, we found that their simple probability-threshold policy, given our regressor, was the stronger policy on FD001, the dataset of their study, but not on FD002 and FD003; we do not claim to match or exceed their reported values, which come from other models on one 20-engine split that is not available to us. Our results add four points: a decomposition of fixed-time versus self-timed evaluation under matched training and calibration; evidence that a window score mainly measures alarm timing; the observation that both window and cost assessments made within batches overstated performance on a held-out batch; and an exact conversion between a cost ratio relative to perfect foresight and \textit{M}, with the effect of the decision grid.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{No field data.} Six of the eight datasets are simulations from one simulator family (C-MAPSS), and the battery and bearing data are laboratory measurements. On the batteries, end of life is a threshold on a measured quantity. First-warning rates of the recipe ranged from 0\% to 98\% across datasets and analyses and fell under a change of test campaign, so they must be re-estimated for each application.
\item \textbf{Development, pre-specification and corrections.} The recipe and grids were developed on PHM08, and the primary warning level was designated after the C-MAPSS results were known. Only three datasets had protocols fixed in advance; the hashes were not deposited with a registry, so their timing rests on the authors' records. The training-only $\kappa$ and the battery analyses with a common physical task and fully nested preprocessing are corrections made after the protocol analyses. The matched decompositions, the timing controls, the capacity baselines, the cost analyses, the shift study and the comparison with \textit{M} are exploratory analyses added after the main results were known; for the most recent of them the plan was hashed before the run. The bearing analysis and the battery timing controls and shift study are not fully nested (Section 5.2).
\item \textbf{Comparison with M.} It is a re-implementation without the reference code, splits or prognostic models; their published version should be checked against the preprint used here for any change to the policy definitions. Their policies need a failure probability, which we built from our regressor in two ways, and the verdicts differed between the two. Our alarms fire between decision times and had to be mapped to a grid of 10 cycles, which their policies use by construction; the grid also changes the perfect reference, by about 2\% on the C-MAPSS datasets and by more than 7\% on N-CMAPSS, which we therefore excluded. Only the replacement-only setting without lead time was examined, the cost ratios were chosen to span the ranges shown in their figures, and their caution about evaluations on 20 test units applies to our thirty random splits and to any comparison with their reported values. FD002--FD004 were evaluated by five-fold cross-validation only.
\item \textbf{Scope of the decomposition.} The matched comparison of Q1 uses two datasets of one simulator family (PHM08 and FD004), one fixed-time model and one recipe. Differences of a few points could go undetected with about 220--250 engines.
\item \textbf{Competitors and baselines.} The challenge approaches are re-implementations, not the original code, and only the networks' caps and warning settings were tuned. The health-indicator, calibrated-indicator, capacity-threshold and capacity-trend baselines are single representatives of their classes, and the capacity trend exists only for the batteries.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} Intervals are conditional on the fitted policies; refitting and fold-assignment variability is reflected only in the repeated PHM08 and FD001 splits and the $\kappa$ sensitivity. Non-significant differences are not evidence of equivalence. Multiplicity is adjusted within tables, not across the paper, and the cost cells or cost ratios within a dataset are correlated, so they are not replications. The interval of their Eq. 7 ignores the variability of the perfect reference and was not used for our verdicts.
\item \textbf{Cost model.} The renewal--reward model assumes a fixed lead time, independent units and replacement as good as new. The cost ratios are illustrative, and the model ignores spare parts, downtime and the grouping of maintenance across units \cite{depater2022,mitici2023}. The window-tuned and cost-tuned policies select on the same out-of-fold predictions that tune their other settings.
\item \textbf{Theory.} Proposition 1 holds for a fixed error distribution. For the sequential policy it is only an approximate description on these data, with errors of up to 8.0 points within $\pm$4 of the selected shift, not a guarantee.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future work}\label{sec:9}

Five directions follow from the limitations. \textbf{Decision-aware alarms.} The trigger could choose its warning level, or its threshold on a failure probability, for the decision times and lead time of the maintenance task, and be compared with the policies of Kamariotis et al. \cite{kamariotis2024} on their ordering setting with lead time. \textbf{Field data and changing conditions.} Alarms tuned on one battery batch lost accuracy on another. Recalibrating the shift on the first failures of a new population, or attaching distribution-free uncertainty to the alarm time, should be tested on fleet data with censoring. \textbf{Maintenance policies.} The alarms of several units could be grouped into shared visits \cite{depater2022,mitici2023}, with the trend and window comparisons repeated under those policies. \textbf{Test and inspection policies.} For units that are proof-tested or inspected at intervals \cite{dejonge2020}, an alarm could trigger an additional test rather than a replacement; this requires an encoder for sparse observations and a cost model with test costs. \textbf{Fault detection.} For failures that become visible only shortly before they happen, the first-warning rule should be compared with sequential detectors such as cumulative-sum and exponentially weighted moving-average charts \cite{page1954,roberts1959} under the same leakage-safe protocol. A two-stage design, in which a detector declares the onset of a defect and a remaining-life model then times the replacement, remains an untested hypothesis for the bearings.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:10}

Scoring remaining-life models only at evaluator-chosen times hides how their alarms behave. Under matched training data and cross-fitted calibration, most of the contrast between fixed-time and self-timed evaluation was due to the observation time (on PHM08, 24.8\% of estimates in the window at half of life against 85.3\% at 90\% of life for the same calibrated model; on FD004, 18.9\% against 66.7\%), and self-timing added no measurable accuracy. The asymmetric window mainly rewarded alarm timing: a constant estimate at the same alarm times scored within 3.6 percentage points of the model's estimate, and the score did not reveal the notice given or the cost incurred. Window-tuned alarms were cheaper than age replacement on five of six engine datasets, but direct cost tuning was better on some datasets and worse on others, and under the decision-oriented metric of Kamariotis et al., re-implemented with their decision grid, their probability-threshold policy built on our regressor had a lower metric value than our window-tuned alarm on FD001 (on FD001 at \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} = 0.1, 5.69\% against 8.08\%) but a higher one on FD002 and FD003, and no policy was best on every dataset. On FD001, the dataset of their study, our alarms did not match that approach. In post hoc analyses of battery cells with fully nested preprocessing, the recipe placed 95.1\% of first warnings in the window within batches and 79.7\% with a batch held out (63.4--84.6\% across fixed time scales), while a capacity-trend baseline placed 98.4\% with a batch held out and was cheaper in most cost settings. On N-CMAPSS the recipe reached 68.7\%, and on bearings it failed (0 of 17). These are results from simulations and laboratory tests; they support evaluating alarms by timing, notice and decision cost, on held-out batches or campaigns, against simple domain baselines and against decision rules that use the decision times explicitly, not the deployment of the recipe.
