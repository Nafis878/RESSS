\section*{S1. Fixed-time reference models}

\noindent For Fig. 2a of the main text, the models were evaluated at 30, 50, 70 and 90\% of each test engine's life in the same outer folds. They were trained on the fit engines (75\% of each outer training part); stacking and the calibration shifts used only the calibration engines (the other 25\%). This fit-75\%/calibrate-25\% protocol is kept as a reference; the matched comparison of Section 6.1 (Table 5 of the main text) instead fits every variant on all outer training engines and calibrates on cross-fitted out-of-fold predictions, using the gradient-boosted model below. The eleven models at half of life are: age only; an input--output hidden Markov model; the similarity model; gradient-boosted trees; a temporal convolutional network with and without augmentation by a conditional generative adversarial network; a hybrid Transformer with an engineered-feature branch; the convolutional and LSTM networks; a physics-informed Bayesian exponential-degradation model with a population prior in the style of Gebraeel et al. [21]; and a stacked hybrid of ten base models and the cycle count, whose quantile stacker and shift were learned on calibration engines pooled over life stages. At half of life the ten single models placed the following shares of their raw estimates in [\textminus{}13, +2], and the stacked hybrid, whose shift was learned on calibration engines, the share of its shifted estimates: age only 11.9\%, hidden Markov model 6.4\%, similarity 24.8\%, gradient-boosted trees 20.6\%, TCN 16.5\%, TCN with GAN augmentation 18.3\%, hybrid Transformer 21.1\%, CNN 24.8\%, LSTM 30.3\%, Bayesian model 26.6\%, stacked hybrid 33.5\%. With a shift chosen on the calibration engines, the five models of Table S1 placed at most 27.5\%. The cross-fitted gradient-boosted model of the matched comparison (Table 5 of the main text) is a separate fit and placed 24.8\%.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Age only}: the mean remaining life of the training engines that survived beyond the current cycle.
\item \textbf{Gradient-boosted trees}: histogram gradient boosting with absolute-error loss (400 iterations, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, minimum 40 samples per leaf, no target cap) on features of the seven temperature and speed sensors (exponential mean with span 5, expanding level, slope and standard deviation, a pooled health index, early-life baseline and age).
\item \textbf{Input--output hidden Markov model}: three health states and an absorbing failure state, four discrete symbols from a principal-component quantisation, one transition matrix per operating regime; remaining life = expected time to absorption.
\item \textbf{LSTM and CNN}: as in Section S15.
\item \textbf{Similarity}: principal-component trajectories matched to all training trajectories over a 60-cycle window; the remaining lives of the 30 closest matches are kernel-weighted into a distribution.
\item \textbf{Physics-informed Bayesian model}: a linear health index with a path a + exp($\theta$ + $\beta$t) and a failure level D, with a multivariate normal population prior; posterior by importance sampling (20,000 samples) conditioned on survival.
\item \textbf{Hybrid Transformer, TCN, TCN with GAN augmentation, stacked hybrid}: quantile models as described in the reproduction package.
\end{itemize}

\section*{S2. Proof of Proposition 1}

\noindent If $\varepsilon$ has distribution \textit{F} not depending on \textit{s}, the warning is correct if and only if \textit{a} $\le$ $\varepsilon$ + \textit{s} $\le$ \textit{b}, that is, $\varepsilon$ $\in$ [\textit{a} \textminus{} \textit{s}, \textit{b} \textminus{} \textit{s}], an interval of width \textit{w} = \textit{b} \textminus{} \textit{a}. Hence \textit{P}(\textit{s}) = \textit{F}(\textit{b} \textminus{} \textit{s}) \textminus{} \textit{F}(\textit{a} \textminus{} \textit{s}) $\le$ sup\textsubscript{x} [\textit{F}(\textit{x} + \textit{w}) \textminus{} \textit{F}(\textit{x})] = \textit{C}\textsubscript{w}(\textit{F}), which is part (i).

\noindent For part (ii), let \textit{f} be the density, unimodal and symmetric about \textit{m}, and write \textit{g}(\textit{x}) = \textit{F}(\textit{x} + \textit{w}) \textminus{} \textit{F}(\textit{x}). Then \textit{g}'(\textit{x}) = \textit{f}(\textit{x} + \textit{w}) \textminus{} \textit{f}(\textit{x}). For \textit{x} < \textit{m} \textminus{} \textit{w}/2 the point \textit{x} + \textit{w} is closer to \textit{m} than \textit{x}, so \textit{f}(\textit{x} + \textit{w}) $\ge$ \textit{f}(\textit{x}) and \textit{g} is non-decreasing; for \textit{x} > \textit{m} \textminus{} \textit{w}/2 the reverse holds. Therefore \textit{g} is maximal at \textit{x} = \textit{m} \textminus{} \textit{w}/2, and \textit{g}(\textit{m} \textminus{} \textit{w}/2) = Pr(|$\varepsilon$ \textminus{} \textit{m}| $\le$ \textit{w}/2). The interval [\textit{a} \textminus{} \textit{s}, \textit{b} \textminus{} \textit{s}] is centred at \textit{m} when \textit{s}\textsuperscript{*} = (\textit{a} + \textit{b})/2 \textminus{} \textit{m}; for [\textminus{}13, +2] this is \textminus{}5.5 \textminus{} \textit{m}. \hfill$\blacksquare$

\noindent The proposition concerns a fixed distribution. In the sequential policy, a different shift moves the alarm to a different cycle, where the unshifted error has a different distribution; Section 6.2 of the main text measures this.

\section*{S4. Leakage checks and preprocessing audit}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item (E1) outer and inner unit sets are disjoint in every fold;
\item (E2) causality: for 15 test engines and six random cut-offs each, predictions from truncated histories equal those from full histories;
\item (E3) a negative control at \textit{H} = 10 in outer fold 1 trained on remaining-life labels built from lives permuted across engines;
\item (E4) refitting fold 1 with its stored settings reproduces the stored outcome of each of its test engines at \textit{H} = 10.
\end{itemize}

\noindent All four checks passed on PHM08: disjoint sets (E1); a maximum absolute difference of 0 over 90 truncation checks (E2); permuted lives reduced the fold-1 in-window rate at \textit{H} = 10 from 97.7\% to 15.9\% (E3); exact reproduction of fold 1 (E4).

\noindent Every preprocessing step was checked for use of test units. Regime z-scores and the removal of constant sensors use the training (or fit) units of each split; regimes are obtained by rounding the operating settings, without fitting; the N-CMAPSS channels were fixed in the protocol; bearing condition statistics come from training bearings, pooled when a condition has no training bearing; the threshold grid of the health-indicator alarm, the constant of the constant-estimate control and the capacity thresholds come from training values. The one dependency found was the block length $\kappa$ of the battery and bearing data (Section 5.2 of the main text), which has been corrected, together with a backward fill of missing per-cycle battery values within a cell.

\section*{S5. Battery analyses}

\noindent \textbf{Data curation (fixed before any model was run).} Structural checks required three deviations, recorded below the hashed section of the protocol. (D1) In the first and third batches the cycle at which capacity falls below 80\% of nominal is not in the summary, so a record counts as complete if it runs to the cycle before. (D2) Five cells of the first batch were continued as five cells of the second; the ten records were identified from the providers' loading code and excluded. (D3) Five cells with a finite official life never fall below 0.91 Ah in the cleaned data and were excluded as censored. Two further records have no official cycle life in the providers' cell list and were not used. This leaves 123 cells (36, 43 and 44 from the three batches). A secondary analysis without four channels flagged as noisy by the providers uses 119 cells. The batches were started in May 2017, June 2017 and April 2018, used different rest periods and share almost no charging protocol (of the 41 protocols of the second batch, 2 occur in another batch); their median lives are 772.5, 481.0 and 1005.5 cycles.

\section*{S6. N-CMAPSS: secondary results}

\noindent At \textit{H} = 10 and 15 the recipe reached 74.7\% and 70.7\%. On the official split (39 test engines) it reached 22/39 = 56.4\% at \textit{H} = 20, compared with 43.6\% for the MLP with Kalman filter and 30.8\% for age-only. The median error at \textit{H} = 20 was \textminus{}2.7 cycles; the debounce selected in every fold was \textit{k} = 5. Two deviations are recorded: seed 106 was used for the inner split of the official split, which has no fold number, and the protocol calls the Kalman filter of the MLP competitor constant-velocity, whereas it is the scalar filter with a fixed decrease of one cycle per cycle. In an exploratory re-run with all 14 channels, the rate at \textit{H} = 20 was 65.7\%.

\section*{S7. Bearing analyses}

\noindent The 17 bearings ran under condition 1 (1800 rpm, 4000 N; seven bearings), condition 2 (1650 rpm, 4200 N; seven) and condition 3 (1500 rpm, 5000 N; three). Lives ranged from 230 to 2803 snapshots (median 1428, about 4 h). \textbf{Protocol analysis} ($\kappa$ = 7 from all bearings): 0 of 17 first warnings in the window at \textit{H} = 20 (7 too early, 10 missed); 0 of 11 on the official split. \textbf{Corrected analysis} ($\kappa$ from training bearings: 7, 4, 9, 6, 7 in the five folds, 4 on the official split): 0 of 17 in cross-validation (7 too early, 0 late, 10 missed), 1 of 11 on the official split; age-only 1 and the MLP with Kalman filter 0 of 17 in cross-validation. Folds with $\kappa$ = 7 reproduced the protocol results exactly. With the physical task fixed (\textit{H} = 140 snapshots, window [\textminus{}91, +14] snapshots), $\kappa$ $\in$ \{3, 5, 7, 10, 14\} gave 1, 0, 0, 0, 1 correct first warnings of 17. The pre-specified theory-guided shift (STW-T) gave 1 of 17 in the corrected analysis.

\noindent Table S6 lists the bearings with a diagnosis made after the run with an ad hoc criterion: the number of blocks ($\kappa$ = 7) before failure from which the summed RMS of the two channels stayed above 1.5 times its mean over the first 20 blocks (0: never sustained; 1: only the final block). In 11 of 17 bearings the rise came within the last six blocks (about seven minutes). This is consistent with the hypothesis that, with these features, most failures gave too little notice for a 20-block alarm, but it does not explain the misses and very early alarms on bearings with long degradation; small numbers (13--14 training bearings per fold), three operating conditions and the feature set are competing explanations, and none was tested.

\section*{S15. Identical-protocol comparison and ablation}

\noindent \textbf{Protocol.} In the spirit of the three winning PHM08 approaches we re-implemented a similarity method [10], an LSTM [11, 13] and a multilayer perceptron followed by a scalar Kalman filter [12], and added a convolutional network [14]. All were run through the same trigger, each with its warning settings chosen on calibration engines (25\% of each outer training part; seed 7) and the shift grid \textminus{}15 to +10. An LSTM, a CNN and a hybrid Transformer were also given exactly the recipe's inputs and cap set. The re-implementations are not the original code, and only the networks' caps and warning settings were tuned. In the ablation (PHM08, nested protocol), each variant changes one component of the recipe and re-tunes the rest.

\noindent \textbf{Results.} Under the identical fit-75\%/tune-25\% protocol, the recipe placed 89.4\% of PHM08 first warnings in the window at \textit{H} = 20. Re-implemented LSTM, similarity and CNN approaches with their original inputs placed 64.2\%, 63.3\% and 55.5\% (Holm-adjusted \textit{p} < 0.001 at \textit{H} = 10 and 20). The MLP with Kalman filter, which shares the recipe's feature encoder, placed 82.6\%; the difference was not significant after correction (Holm-adjusted \textit{p} = 0.071 at \textit{H} = 20, 0.240 at \textit{H} = 10). Given the recipe's inputs and cap set, an LSTM and a CNN rose to 80.7\% and 78.9\%, still significantly below the recipe at \textit{H} = 20. A hybrid Transformer reached 92.2\%, numerically higher (13 against 19 discordant engines, \textit{p} = 0.377), at about 36 minutes of training per fold against about 34 seconds. Inputs and targets therefore explain much of the gap to the re-implementations. Since only the networks' caps and warning settings were tuned, these comparisons do not rank architectures.

\noindent \textbf{Ablation.} Removing the shift caused the largest loss among the trigger and target components (77.1\%, with 49 late alarms; Holm-adjusted \textit{p} < 0.001). Training without a cap gave 81.7\% (\textit{p} = 0.002), consistent with the target-design effect in Table 5 of the main text, and an untuned rule gave 78.4\%. Seven sensors gave 83.9\%, sensor features without age 85.8\%, and age alone 14.2\%. The conventional cap of 125 (89.9\%), no smoothing (90.4\%) and no debounce (89.4\%) were not significantly different from the full recipe; with 218 engines such tests can miss differences of a few percentage points.

\section*{S16. Correction note and change log}

\noindent \textbf{Correction note.} In the protocol analyses of the battery and bearing data, the block length $\kappa$ was computed from the median life of all units, including the test units of each split, so that test-unit lives influenced the time scale of the task. An intermediate correction computed $\kappa$ from the outer training units of each split and let the inner folds inherit it, but kept the warning level and the window in blocks, so that the physical task (in cycles or snapshots) changed with $\kappa$. For the battery cells, the analysis reported in the main text fixes the task in cycles (\textit{H} = 60, window [\textminus{}39, +6], lead times 0--45 cycles), computes $\kappa$ separately in every inner training subset and in the outer training set, and reports $\kappa$ chosen by inner cross-validation and fixed $\kappa$ = 2--6 as sensitivity analyses. For the bearings the intermediate correction is retained (Section 5.2 of the main text). The table below gives the results under each convention. The protocol and intermediate results are reported for transparency; the fully nested results are the ones interpreted. Two statements in an earlier version of the manuscript are withdrawn because the shift study does not support them: that no choice of shift could rescue a low in-window rate, and that the concentration of the out-of-fold error could serve as a check before deployment. Any reading of the contrast between fixed-time prediction at half of life and the self-timed alarm as an effect of self-timing is not supported by the matched comparison (Section 6.1 of the main text).

\section*{S17. Comparison with the metric M: implementation, checks and all cost ratios}

\noindent \textbf{Implementation.} The metric was re-implemented from the preprint of Kamariotis et al. (arXiv:2306.03759v2): the cost-rate estimator and its first-order variance (their Eqs. 2--3), the perfect policy at the last decision time before failure (Eq. 4), \textit{M} and its variance (Eqs. 6--7, 9), the heuristic policy 1 (Eq. 10), policy 2 (Eq. 12) and policy 3 with option 1 for the cost rate (Eq. 14). Fifteen unit tests pass, including \textit{M} = 0 for the perfect policy, \textit{M} $\ge$ 0 for 2,000 random grid policies, a hand-computed three-unit example, the recording (not clipping) of negative \textit{M} for an off-grid policy, and the case of their footnote 1 in which letting a unit fail is optimal when \textit{c}\textsubscript{c} is close to \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}. That case did not occur in any simulator setting or dataset--ratio combination. \textbf{Simulator check.} Their virtual RUL simulator (time to failure normal with mean 225 and standard deviation 40, lognormal multiplicative errors with $\sigma$ = 0.4 or 0.15 and correlation length 50, $\Delta$\textit{T} = 10, 2,000 components) gave the qualitative pattern of their Figs. 4--5 (next table): the optimised policy 1 had the lowest \textit{M} at all seven ratios for both $\sigma$, policy 3 was below policy 2, and the optimised threshold lay below \textit{c}\textsubscript{p}/\textit{c}\textsubscript{c} at 6 of 7 ratios for $\sigma$ = 0.15 and 7 of 7 ratios for $\sigma$ = 0.4. As in their study, the threshold was optimised and scored on the same simulated components.

\section*{S19. Time-scale conventions of the battery and bearing analyses}

\noindent \textbf{Fully nested preprocessing (batteries, convention C).} The battery task is defined in physical units (main text Section 3). Within each outer training set, every inner training subset builds its own blocks, its own z-score statistics and its own regressor, with $\kappa$ computed from that subset only. Its inner validation cells are scored in their own blocks against the same physical window, so the out-of-fold counts of the five inner folds can be summed per setting even when their $\kappa$ differ. After selection, blocks, statistics and regressor are rebuilt from the complete outer training set, with $\kappa$ from that set, and the outer test cells are scored once. This is done under cross-validation by cell (five folds, seed 2026) and with each of the three batches held out in turn. Three ways of choosing $\kappa$ are reported: \textbf{rule, nested}, $\kappa$ by the protocol rule computed from each training subset (inner subsets for selection, the outer training set for the final model); \textbf{selected by inner cross-validation}, $\kappa$ $\in$ \{2, \ldots{}, 6\} with the largest summed out-of-fold count (ties to the outer rule value, then to the smaller $\kappa$); and \textbf{fixed}, each $\kappa$ $\in$ \{2, \ldots{}, 6\} in turn, as a sensitivity analysis. No failure life outside a training subset enters its time scale or its selection, so neither the outer test estimates nor the inner selections use held-out lives. Alarms can only be raised at block ends, and the remaining life of a block is that at its last cycle; only the smoothing span and the debounce are counted in blocks.

\noindent \textbf{Battery cost.} The battery cost analysis (main text Tables 8 and 12) is fully nested in the same way, with the task, the lead times and every threshold in cycles; an earlier version that took $\kappa$ from the outer training set and expressed the window-tuned task in blocks (convention B) is kept in Section S11 for provenance. The battery timing controls and shift study (main text Section 6.2) still take $\kappa$ from the outer training cells, with the task in blocks (window [\textminus{}13, +2] blocks, warning levels in blocks).

\noindent \textbf{Bearings.} The bearing analyses use $\kappa$ from the outer training set (inner folds inherit it), and a sensitivity analysis holds the physical task fixed for $\kappa$ $\in$ \{3, 5, 7, 10, 14\}. Inheriting $\kappa$ can influence which settings are selected, but not the outer test estimate. We did not repeat the bearing analysis with full nesting, because every variant run gave at most one correct alarm in 17.

\noindent \textbf{How an operator would choose $\kappa$.} In service, lives are unknown, but three sources of information are not: the lives of units previously run to failure (the training fleet, as here), design or warranty lives, and the notice that a replacement needs. We therefore regard the task as fixed in physical units and $\kappa$ as a temporal resolution, chosen from historical lives or by cross-validation on them. For the batteries the all-cell median life (731 cycles) lies just below the boundary between $\kappa$ = 3 and $\kappa$ = 4 (733.25 cycles), which is why the protocol value and the training-only values differ in some splits.
